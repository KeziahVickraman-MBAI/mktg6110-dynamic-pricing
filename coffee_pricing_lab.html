Concept 01

What is dynamic pricing?

Prices that change along four dimensions, the “4 Ps” of dynamic pricing (Kopalle et al. 2023). These are not the marketing-mix 4 Ps! Click a P to see what it looks like in a coffee shop.

🗣 In plain English

Dynamic pricing just means the price is not fixed. It can change depending on who is buying, what they buy, when they buy and where.

☕ A café that charges more on a rainy Saturday morning than on a quiet Tuesday afternoon is already doing dynamic pricing.

Dynamic pricing is an umbrella term

Why firms adopt it, the downsides, and three real cases

Why firms adopt it

  • Capture more of each customer’s willingness-to-pay (WTP)
  • Allocate scarce inventory efficiently (e.g., the last 40 cups on a Saturday)
  • Personalise the experience and boost engagement
  • Competitive necessity: rivals reprice instantly

Downsides

  • Implementation complexity
  • Need for AI and data expertise
  • Consumer backlash risk
  • Potential legal exposure

How much personalisation is optimal, and defensible?

✈️ Delta

Planned AI revenue management across 20% of its domestic network. After public and lawmaker backlash, it assured US lawmakers that it would not set personalised fares.

🚗 Uber

Surge pricing reached 4× normal fares during the Sydney café siege. Uber later said: “We didn’t stop surge pricing immediately. This was the wrong decision.”

🏢 RealPage

The DOJ alleged that its shared rent-pricing algorithm let landlords coordinate rent increases. The 2024 suit was followed by a settlement.

Takeaway. “One price for everyone” was itself an innovation that built trust. Data and algorithms are now moving pricing back toward the individual, which is powerful and risky in equal measure.

Concept 02 · Periods & places

The best price moves with the conditions

This is the same demand model the Lab uses. Each walk-in buys if the coffee is worth more to them than the price, compared against the café across the street. Change the conditions and watch the profit-maximising price move.

🗣 In plain English

Raise the price and fewer people buy. Lower it and you sell more, but earn less on each cup. The best price is the sweet spot in between, and that sweet spot moves when the day changes.

☕ Rain, a weekend crowd or a festival makes people keen for coffee, so the sweet spot goes up. A cheaper café across the road pulls it down.

Concept 03 · People

Uniform price vs. personalised prices: who wins?

1,000 simulated morning customers: students (lower WTP) and professionals (higher WTP). Compare one price for all, a price per segment, and an AI that estimates each person’s WTP. The baseline is always the best possible uniform price.

🗣 In plain English

One price for everyone leaves money on the table: some people would have paid more, and some walk away. Charging each person what they are willing to pay earns the café more. Many low-budget customers actually gain, while people who would pay a lot end up paying more.

☕ A student who would only pay $3 gets a $3 coffee instead of none. The lawyer who would happily pay $6 now pays $5.50 instead of $4.

Concept 04 · Data & algorithms

From heuristics to machine learning to reinforcement learning

How do we turn all those data points (weather, events, competitor prices, inventory) into a price? Here are three generations of pricing logic. Build your own rule and race it against the other two over 100 simulated three-week periods.

🗣 In plain English

A rule of thumb is a recipe you write yourself. ML is a forecaster: it predicts today and picks today’s best price. RL is a learner: it tries things over and over and learns which price works in each situation, including how today affects tomorrow.

☕ Rule of thumb: “raise prices when stock is low.” ML: “today looks busy, so charge $5.” RL: “busy Friday, but Saturday will be busier, so save some cups.”

Naïve

Heuristic rule

“If inventory < X, raise the price by Y%.” Simple and transparent, but blind to the patterns it was never told about.

Machine learning

Predict, then optimise

Forecast today’s demand from the features, then pick the price that maximises today’s profit. Each prediction is independent.

Reinforcement learning

Learn a policy

Learn by trial and error which price works in each state, including how today’s sales change tomorrow’s inventory.

Why beating the RL agent should not be the point (6 reasons)
🏋️

Unequal practice

The agent trained on 20,000 episodes × 21 days = 420,000 simulated days, about 1,150 years of café mornings. You get 21. Losing to it says nothing about your judgement, and beating it once says little either.

🎲

One game is mostly noise

Weather, events and rival prices swing a week’s profit far more than small pricing skill does. As the test above shows, even a fixed plan beats RL on some weeks. A single win or loss is largely the dice.

🎯

Goodhart’s law

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Chase the bot’s score and you start fitting this scenario (hindsight, gaming the rules) instead of learning how demand works. The score goes up and the understanding doesn’t.

🧭

The manager’s job is different

Algorithms optimise the reward they are given, inside the world they were trained on. People choose that reward (profit or loyalty?), set guardrails (no surge pricing in an emergency, no discrimination), notice when the world changes (a new rival, a siege) and explain prices to customers and regulators.

🔁

What the game is really for

It lets you feel the RL loop: observe, hypothesise, explore or exploit, get a reward, update. The exercise asks how you learned, not whether you won. Your thinking journal in the Game companion tab is the evidence.

🤝

The better question

Not “human or algorithm?” but “how do I direct one?” The ZipRecruiter study found the big gain was setting the right price level, a managerial decision, before any personalisation algorithm was switched on.

Takeaway. ML is easy to beat because it is myopic: it maximises each day alone and spends stock the weekend needed. RL is hard to beat because it plans across days and has practised more than any human could. So treat the RL agent as a benchmark to learn from, not an opponent: ask what its prices reveal about demand, and focus on how your own policy improves.

Concept 06 · Reinforcement learning

The RL vocabulary, in a coffee shop

RL is machine learning where an agent learns by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties. The objective is to maximise long-term cumulative reward. Click a term, or simulate a day to watch the loop run.

🗣 In plain English

RL is learning by trial and error. The learner looks at the situation, tries something, gets a reward (or not), and slowly figures out what works best in each situation.

☕ Like training a puppy: sit → treat. Our “puppy” is the pricing algorithm, and the treat is the day’s profit.

The learning process

1 · Observe the environment→2 · Take an action→3 · Receive feedback→4 · Improve future decisions↺
Takeaway. Nobody tells the agent the demand curve. It sees a state, tries a price, collects a reward and nudges its estimate of how good that price is in that state. The Lab’s Train the agent tab lets you watch every one of those nudges.

Concept 07 · The fundamental trade-off

Explore vs. exploit

Imagine an ordinary Tuesday: you don’t know which price earns the most, and each day’s profit is noisy. Exploring tries new prices to gather information. Exploiting uses the best price found so far. Run your own experiment, or let an ε-greedy agent do it.

🗣 In plain English

Do you order your favourite dish again (exploit) or try something new that might be even better (explore)? Too much of either costs you. Good learners try new things early and settle down later.

☕ Testing a $6 latte once is how you find out whether customers would pay it. Testing it every day just loses sales.

Real-world example. A business tests new pricing strategies while continuing proven ones. With ε = 0 the agent can lock onto the first price that looked good. With ε = 1 it never benefits from what it learned. Most good learners explore early and exploit later, which is what the Lab agent does by decaying ε.

Concept 08 · How RL is different

Sequential decisions: today’s price changes tomorrow’s options

In traditional ML each prediction is independent. In RL, current decisions affect future opportunities, rewards and states. Here you have one delivery of beans for Friday and Saturday, and a farmers’ market comes to town on Saturday.

🗣 In plain English

Cups you sell today cannot be sold tomorrow. If tomorrow is busier and people will pay more, it is worth charging a bit more today to save stock.

☕ Selling your last 50 cups cheaply on Friday means turning away Saturday’s festival crowd, who would have paid more.

Saturday’s price is then set optimally for whatever stock is left. Unsold cups are discarded at the $1.20 cost.

Profit as a function of Friday’s price
🔎 Unpack what’s happening
Takeaway. A myopic optimiser (the ML suggestion) maximises Friday alone and sells cups that would have earned more on Saturday. A sequential planner gives up a little Friday profit for a larger total. Slide the stock up: once inventory is plentiful the gap nearly disappears, because the future no longer constrains today.

Concept 09 · Legality & acceptance

Fairness, framing and the law

Firms’ profit is only half the story. Customers judge fairness against a reference price, regulators are writing disclosure rules, and shared pricing algorithms can slide into collusion.

🗣 In plain English

People do not just judge the price, they judge whether it is fair. A discount feels like a gift, while a higher personal price feels like being targeted, even for the same amount. Laws are starting to require shops to say when an algorithm used personal data to set a price.

☕ “You saved $0.60!” feels nice. “You pay $0.90 more than the last person” feels awful. It is the same coffee and the same $5.40.

Environment: the coffee market

  • 21 days = 3 weeks, starting on a Monday. Base walk-in traffic: Mon 110 · Tue 100 · Wed 105 · Thu 115 · Fri 140 · Sat 185 · Sun 165.
  • Weather: sunny (45%), cloudy (25%), rainy (20%: fewer walk-ins, but they want coffee more), cold snap (10%: WTP up most).
  • Events (10% on weekdays, 30% Fri–Sun): +50% traffic and higher WTP.
  • Weekend customers are less price-sensitive (leisure, not commuters).
  • Competitor “Bean There” opens on 50% of days, priced at $3.00–$5.50.
  • Each walk-in chooses our café, the competitor or nothing (logit choice):
    P(buy here) = eu − 0.9·p / (1 + eu − 0.9·p + eu + 0.2 − 0.9·pcomp) where u rises with weekend, weather and events. Daily demand also has ±10% random noise.

Reward & inventory

  • Every Monday you receive 450 cups of beans. Variable cost: $1.20 / cup sold.
  • If demand exceeds stock you sell what you have, and each turned-away customer costs $0.50 in goodwill.
  • Beans left on Sunday night are discarded: −$1.20 / cup.
  • Net reward = revenue − cost of cups sold − stockout penalty − waste.

The competitors you face

  • RL agent: tabular Q-learning, pre-trained on 20,000 random 21-day episodes (α = 0.05, γ = 0.5, ε 1 → 0.02). It plays greedily.
  • ML suggestion: perfect demand forecast for today, maximises today’s profit, and ignores inventory and the future.
  • Static menu: $4.50 every day.
  • Hindsight optimum: a planner that knew every day’s demand in advance (dynamic programming). A ceiling, not a real competitor.

How the RL agent sees the world (state)

The agent does not see raw numbers. It sees 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 states:

  • Demand: Low / Normal / High, from day of week × weather × event.
  • Competitor: None / Cheaper (< $4) / Pricier (≥ $4).
  • Stock: Tight / OK / Plenty, cups left compared with the share of the week still to come.

Actions: 19 prices, $1.00 to $10.00 in $0.50 steps. You can fine-tune to $0.25, which is one small edge you have over it.

Q-learning in one line

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α · [ r + γ · maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]
  • r + γ·max Q(s′,·) is the “target”: today’s reward plus the discounted value of the best move tomorrow.
  • The bracket is the surprise, or temporal-difference error. α sets how much of the surprise is absorbed.
  • Policy = in each state, pick the price with the highest Q (exploit), except with probability ε pick at random (explore).
